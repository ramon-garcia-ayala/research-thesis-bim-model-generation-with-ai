## 2026-11-15 · Summary
Chen, Han, Hu, Ruiz and Liu (Tufts University and DeepMind, ICML 2021). Autoregressive graph generators add nodes and
edges one at a time, and to train them each graph is fixed to a single node ordering, usually BFS or DFS. The paper
shows that this does not correspond to maximum likelihood, because a graph is the same under any permutation of its
nodes and many sequences lead to it, so p(G) would be the sum over all orderings, which is intractable. The authors
derive the exact joint probability of the graph and the node ordering (including the role of graph automorphisms), and
treat the ordering as a latent variable: a neural network (a GNN) infers a distribution over orderings for each graph,
and the generator is trained on orderings sampled from it by variational inference, maximising a lower bound of log
p(G). The log-likelihood is estimated by importance sampling. Applied to three existing generators (DeepGMG, GraphRNN
and GraphGEN) on six datasets: Community-small (500 graphs, 12 to 20 nodes), Citeseer-small (200), Enzymes (563),
Lung (400), Yeast (400) and Cora (400).

Results
- Likelihood (Table 1): the VI method shows better predictive performance on most datasets, often very significantly
  (paired t-test at 5%), and its lower bound is tighter than the bound of the previous schemes. On Yeast it is very
  close to the DeepGMG baseline; the authors checked that the sampled orderings there are very similar to DFS orders.
- Quality of samples (Table 2, maximum mean discrepancy on degree, clustering and 4-node orbits, lower is better):
  the VI method improves all three models on four datasets (Community-small, Enzymes, Yeast and Cora), with some minor
  drops on the other two. On Citeseer-small only one metric drops, for GraphRNN and GraphGEN.
- Bias of BFS (Fig. 3): on Community-small the graphs are two communities joined by one edge. The model trained with BFS
  orderings makes two communities but does not usually join them with a single edge, the one trained with uniform
  orderings does not make two communities, and the VI one captures the pattern with one exception. The averaged
  adjacency matrices show the BFS order leaving two blocks near zero; on Enzymes, the learned orderings give band
  matrices while BFS scatters the non-zeros over a wider range.
- Cost: O(S n |E|) per gradient with S = 4 samples in the experiments, which the authors give as the main limitation:
  it does not scale to large graphs.

For the thesis
- It gives the reasoning behind placement-order-experiments: when a model places elements one by one, the order is a
  hidden choice that changes what it learns, and a fixed BFS can put a pattern into the generated graphs that is not in
  the data (Fig. 3). A Revit model has no canonical order of its elements, so the choice cannot be avoided.
- It argues for learning the order and not fixing it, which is a fourth option next to the three orders of the thesis,
  but a costly one: it is stated to not scale to large graphs, and the datasets are graphs of 5 to 125 nodes.
- The graphs are small (5 to 125 nodes) and the experiments are on structure, so it says nothing about Revit elements
  with geometry and parameters. No comparison with parallel generation.
- It evaluates by similarity to a test set (MMD) and likelihood, which rewards matching the data and is not a test of
  new against copied; the paper says only that the generated graphs "match the structures of target graphs not seen
  during training".
