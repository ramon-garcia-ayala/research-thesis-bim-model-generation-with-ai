## 2026-11-15 · Summary
Ward, Wang and Cheng (UCLA, arXiv preprint 2406.13012, 2024). The paper proposes the Data Plagiarism Index (DPI), a
measure of data-copying for tabular generative models, and a membership inference attack built on it (DPI MIA). For
each training record x, take its K nearest neighbours in the pooled space of reference records and synthetic records;
DPI(x) is the number of synthetic records in that neighbourhood divided by the number of reference records. DPI = 0
means no synthetic point nearby (possible under-fitting), DPI = 1 means as many synthetic as reference points (no
copying, no under-fitting), and DPI > 1 means more synthetic than reference points, with larger values pointing to
plagiarism. For the attack, the original dataset is split into three equal parts (training, holdout and reference), the
model is trained on the first and generates a synthetic set of the same size, DPI is computed for the training and the
holdout records, and a record is predicted to be a member if its DPI is above a threshold. It is tested on the Adult
Census dataset with several tabular generators (Tab-DDPM among them), replicated 5 times; K = 20 with L2 distance is
used for the plots.

Results
- Fig. 4: models that perform better on utility (a classifier AUCROC trained on the synthetic set, maximum mean
  discrepancy and Wasserstein distance against the training data) have a higher DPI MIA AUCROC, that is a higher risk of
  copying; the authors say that it is not known whether this is causal or only a correlation.
- Fig. 1 and Sec. 5.3: the top 1% of training records by DPI for Tab-DDPM sit in an outlier region of the t-SNE plot
  and are mostly married, white, middle-aged respondents with high capital gains, in private industry, who earn
  more than 50k, which the authors read as a fairness concern.
- Fig. 5 and Table 1: DPI MIA is competitive with other membership inference attacks across training-set sizes, "dominating
  at most testing sizes"; GAN Leaks Calibrated performs similarly or better in places, but the Pearson correlation
  between its scores and DPI's is relatively low, so DPI picks up a different kind of copying. The authors say the aim is
  not a state-of-the-art attack.
- DPI is "fairly stable" for different K and distance metrics, with the details of the ablation left to an appendix
  whose reference is broken in the text.

For the thesis
- Of the five copy tests in the library it is the one for mixed-type tables, which is how the attributes of a Revit
  element look (a category, a type name, numbers). Its training set is one table of records of the same kind, not a
  graph of related elements, so a distance for elements joined by relations is still the thesis's job.
- The local view is a useful idea: instead of one score for the whole generator, a score for each training item. It
  could say which of the 9 training models a generated model copies and where.
- It needs three disjoint parts of the data: training, holdout and reference. With a plan of ten models, that cuts
  an already small set, and K = 20 neighbours would be more than the models there are. The paper uses the Adult
  dataset, split in three equal parts, and does not discuss small sets.
- It measures privacy risk, not novelty: the question there is whether a record of the training set can be recovered,
  while the thesis asks whether the new model is not a copy. The two overlap in the check of closeness to the nearest
  training item, but the paper does not use the word "novelty".
- A preprint without peer review, and no test of significance for DPI values (the null problem in majhi-2026).
